About

Starting a robotics team from nothing

Millis doesn't have a robotics team. Westwood, Franklin, Walpole, and Nobles & Greenough all do. This is the effort to change that — and a record of everything it takes.

Mission

Give Millis students a shot at competitive engineering.

Robotics is the closest thing school offers to actual engineering work: an open-ended problem, a hard deadline, a limited budget, and a competition at the end where the thing either works or it doesn't.

Millis students who want that experience currently have to go looking for it somewhere else. Our goal is a team that competes in FIRST Tech Challenge — and that keeps running after its founders graduate. That means building the boring parts properly on the way in: a mentor, a meeting space, a budget, sponsors, and a roster of students who actually show up.

It also means being realistic. We'll be a rookie team going up against programs that have been running for twenty years. Success in year one is a functional robot at a real competition, not a trophy.

Team at a glance

  • Program: FIRST Tech Challenge (FTC)
  • Season: 2026–27, FIRST CANOPY presented by BIOBUZZ

The program

What FIRST Tech Challenge actually is

A new game is announced every September. Teams have a few months to design and build a robot that plays it, then compete at qualifiers over the winter.

One game, one robot

The season's challenge drops at kickoff in September. Matches pair you with an alliance partner, and every match starts with a 30-second autonomous period where the robot runs on its own code.

Judged on more than the robot

Teams keep an engineering notebook and are judged on design process, outreach, and teamwork — so a rookie team with a modest robot can still win awards.

Scholarships attached

FIRST participation unlocks a large scholarship catalog, and a lot of it goes unclaimed every year simply because students don't apply.

How we got here

The story so far

  1. May 2026

    Map the neighbours

    Started by finding every school near Millis that fields a robotics team, then digging into how each program is funded and organized — and emailing them to ask how they got started.

  2. June 2026

    Learn from a rookie team

    A long call with a student who launched a rookie FTC team the year before: three committed students is enough to start, year one ran about $4,000, and the costs he forgot were food and transportation.

  3. June 2026

    Talk to FIRST Massachusetts

    The state program staff walked us through the real calendar, the grant landscape, free mentor training, and one expensive misconception: FTC provides no kit. Teams buy everything.

  4. August 2026

    Build the paperwork

    A full year-one budget with sourced prices, a student interest survey, and outreach to school staff and the wider Millis community looking for a mentor.

  5. September 2026

    Kickoff

    The season's game is revealed on Saturday, September 12. Whatever the roster looks like by then, we're going.

Still needed

What's blocking us

A community mentor. One adult with a making, engineering, or coding background, a few hours a week. FIRST provides free training — no FTC experience required.

A place to meet. A room we can leave a half-built robot in, a few days a week.

Sponsors. Roughly $5,500 for a full first season, and no team gets there without local support.

Students. Three committed people is the real minimum. More is better.

Commitment

What joining actually asks of you

FTC teams scale to what they've got. Here are the two ends of the range, straight from teams already running in Massachusetts.

Competitive

Four evenings and a weekend day

About eight students, three or four dedicated mentors, meeting four nights a week plus a weekend day in season. This is what the teams that win look like.

Minimum viable

One hour a week

Three core students and one adult mentor, an hour a week, is a legitimate team that competes. Year one for Millis will land somewhere between these two — set by who signs up.

Meeting times will be chosen around what students actually pick on the interest survey — before school, after school, or weekends.

Questions

Frequently asked

Do I need robotics experience to join?

No. Most people on a rookie team have never built a robot before — that's what a rookie team is. If you're willing to learn and show up, you're qualified. There's also room for people who'd rather write, design, or handle sponsorship and outreach than turn a wrench.

Does it cost money to be on the team?

The team's costs are covered by fundraising, sponsors, and grants — that's the entire point of building the budget first. If cost is ever a barrier for a student who wants to join, tell us and we'll work it out.

What does being a mentor involve?

Being the experienced adult in the room. You don't design the robot, you don't need to know FTC, and you don't have to teach a class — students do the building. FIRST publishes free mentor training, including a Mentor-Ready track and technical modules, so you're not starting cold. The commitment is a few hours a week and it's unpaid.

Is this an official Millis school team?

Not yet. This is a student-founded effort working toward school support for space, funding, and travel to competitions. FTC teams can be school-based or community-based, so the season doesn't hinge on any single approval — but school backing makes everything easier, and that's the goal.

Where are competitions, and how far is the travel?

Massachusetts events cluster east of Worcester — mostly the Boston area, with occasional trips further out. From Millis that's generally under an hour. Each team is offered two qualifying events between December and March, with the state championship in late March.

How do I sponsor or donate?

Email us and we'll send the sponsorship details, including exactly which budget line your contribution covers. Sponsor names go on the back of the team shirt and on this site.
