About

A competition team, run by students

Millis Robotics competes in the VEX V5 Robotics Competition — the same game, the same deadline, and the same field as every other team in the state.

What we do

Engineering with a scoreboard attached.

Robotics is the closest thing school offers to real engineering work: an open-ended problem, a hard deadline, a limited budget, and a competition at the end where the thing either works or it doesn’t.

Students on this team do the whole job. They read the game rules and argue about strategy. They model the robot in CAD, cut and assemble it, wire the V5 control system, and write the code that drives it. They keep an engineering notebook that documents every decision, and they stand in front of judges to defend it.

Then they drive it — two-team alliances, fifteen seconds of autonomous play, and a minute and forty-five of everything they practiced.

The competition

How VEX works

One game per season, played by every VEX team in the world, revealed each spring and competed all the way through to the world championship.

The matches

Two-team alliances face off on a 12-foot field. Every match opens with a fully autonomous period before drivers take over — so the code matters as much as the driving.

Skills challenges

One minute driving, one minute fully autonomous, no opponent. Scores are ranked against every team in the world, so you always know where you stand.

Awards

Judges give out awards for design, innovation, and teamwork — and the top one, Excellence, is won on the engineering notebook as much as the robot.

A year on the team

What the season looks like

  1. Spring

    The game drops

    Next season’s game is revealed at the world championship. Everybody starts from zero on the same day.

  2. Summer

    Strategy and prototypes

    Read the rules, argue about scoring, and build rough prototypes of the mechanisms that might work.

  3. Fall

    Build and drive

    The real robot gets built, programmed, and driven — and the first tournaments arrive fast.

  4. Winter

    Tournament season

    Events across Massachusetts, iterating on the robot between each one, chasing a spot at states.

  5. Late winter – spring

    States, then Worlds

    The Massachusetts state championship, and the world championship for teams that qualify.

Commitment

How much time it takes

In season: a couple of afternoons a week, more in the run-up to a tournament, plus the occasional Saturday for the event itself.

Competition days are full days — and they are the best part of the year.

Exact meeting times get set around what students pick on the sign-up form, so the schedule fits real lives with sports, jobs, and homework in them.

Questions

Frequently asked

Do I need robotics experience to join?

No. Most people arrive having never built a robot, and the team teaches as it goes. If you are willing to learn and to show up, you are qualified. There is also plenty of room for people who would rather write, design, film, or handle sponsorship than turn a wrench.

What does it cost a student to join?

Nothing. The team’s costs are covered by sponsors and fundraising. If any expense ever becomes a barrier for a student who wants to be here, tell us and we will sort it out.

How much time does it take?

A couple of afternoons a week in season, ramping up before tournaments, plus competition Saturdays. Meeting times are set around what students choose on the sign-up form.

What do I actually learn?

CAD, machining and assembly, wiring, programming in VEXcode, and the habit of documenting a design so somebody else can follow it. Also the part nobody advertises: how to fix something under time pressure while it matters.

Where are competitions?

Massachusetts hosts tournaments across the state through the fall and winter, and event listings go up on RobotEvents as they are scheduled. The state championship is in late winter, and VEX Worlds in the spring for teams that qualify.

How can adults help?

Volunteer, or sponsor. Volunteers do not need to know VEX — being an experienced adult in the room is the job. Sponsors keep the robot funded, and get their name on the shirt.
